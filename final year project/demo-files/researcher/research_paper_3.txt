FEDERATED LEARNING FOR PRIVACY-PRESERVING MEDICAL
IMAGE CLASSIFICATION IN DISTRIBUTED HOSPITAL NETWORKS

ABSTRACT

Medical data sharing across hospitals is severely restricted by privacy
regulations (HIPAA, GDPR), limiting the development of robust AI diagnostic
models. Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative model training without
sharing raw patient data, making it ideal for healthcare applications. This
paper presents FedMed, a federated learning framework for chest X-ray
pneumonia classification across a simulated network of 8 hospitals with
non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) data. Using FedAvg
with differential privacy (DP-SGD), FedMed achieves 94.1% accuracy —
comparable to centralized training (95.2%) — while maintaining strong
privacy guarantees (ε=2.0, δ=1e-5). The framework is evaluated for
communication efficiency, model convergence, and robustness to Byzantine
faults (malicious clients).

1. INTRODUCTION

AI has demonstrated transformative potential in medical imaging, with deep
learning models matching or exceeding radiologist performance for conditions
like pneumonia, diabetic retinopathy, and skin cancer. However, training
robust models requires large, diverse datasets that are rarely available
in a single institution.

Data sharing between hospitals is challenging due to:
- Patient privacy regulations (HIPAA in US, PDPB in India)
- Institutional competitive concerns
- Data heterogeneity across equipment and populations
- High communication overhead

Federated Learning (McMahan et al., 2017) addresses this by keeping data
local and sharing only model gradients/weights.

1.1 Problem Statement

Existing federated learning approaches assume IID data distribution across
clients, which is unrealistic in medical settings where different hospitals
see different patient populations and disease prevalences. Additionally,
most FL frameworks lack formal privacy guarantees against gradient inversion
attacks.

1.2 Research Objectives

1. Implement FedAvg with differential privacy for chest X-ray classification
2. Evaluate performance under non-IID data distributions
3. Analyze communication efficiency vs. model accuracy trade-offs
4. Test Byzantine fault tolerance against malicious client attacks

2. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

McMahan et al. (2017) introduced FedAvg, the foundational FL algorithm
that aggregates local model updates via weighted averaging.

Li et al. (2020) identified the "statistical heterogeneity challenge" in
FL, showing accuracy drops of 5-10% in non-IID settings. FedProx was
proposed as a solution with proximal term regularization.

Dwork et al. (2014) formalized differential privacy (DP) theory, providing
mathematical guarantees against membership inference attacks.

Shokri et al. (2021) demonstrated gradient inversion attacks where raw
training images can be reconstructed from shared gradients — highlighting
critical privacy vulnerabilities in naive FL implementations.

2.1 Identified Research Gaps

- No existing study combines DP-SGD + Byzantine fault tolerance for medical FL
- Non-IID performance degradation in hospital networks not systematically studied
- Communication overhead of DP mechanisms on bandwidth-limited hospital networks
- Lack of evaluation on Indian hospital datasets (geographical gap)

3. METHODOLOGY

3.1 Dataset

Chest X-Ray Images (Pneumonia) — Kaggle/Guangzhou Women and Children Hospital:
- Total images: 5,863 (Pneumonia: 4,273, Normal: 1,590)
- Split across 8 simulated hospital clients using Dirichlet distribution (α=0.5)
  to simulate non-IID data
- Each client: 500-900 training images, 80-150 validation images

3.2 Model Architecture

Base: DenseNet-121 (pre-trained on ImageNet)
- Input: 224x224x3
- Custom head: GlobalAvgPool → Dropout(0.3) → FC(2) → Sigmoid
- Binary classification: Pneumonia vs. Normal

3.3 Federated Learning Setup

- Algorithm: FedAvg with DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016)
- Privacy budget: ε=2.0, δ=1e-5
- Noise multiplier: σ=1.1
- Gradient clipping: C=1.0
- Communication rounds: 100
- Local epochs per round: 3
- Client fraction: 0.75 (6/8 clients per round)
- Aggregation: Weighted FedAvg (by local dataset size)

3.4 Byzantine Fault Tolerance

- Attack simulated: 1-2 malicious clients sending random gradients
- Defense: Robust aggregation using Krum algorithm
- Evaluation: Accuracy before/after attack under Krum vs. naive FedAvg

4. RESULTS

4.1 Accuracy Comparison

| Approach                    | Accuracy | AUC   | Privacy |
|----------------------------|----------|-------|---------|
| Centralized (no privacy)   | 95.2%    | 0.974 | None    |
| Local only (no federation) | 87.3%    | 0.891 | High    |
| FedAvg (no DP)             | 93.8%    | 0.961 | Low     |
| FedMed (FedAvg + DP-SGD)   | 94.1%    | 0.958 | Strong  |

4.2 Non-IID Performance (α=0.5 vs α=0.1)

| Distribution | Accuracy | Convergence (rounds) |
|-------------|----------|---------------------|
| IID         | 94.8%    | 42                  |
| Non-IID α=0.5| 94.1%   | 67                  |
| Non-IID α=0.1| 91.3%   | 89                  |

4.3 Byzantine Fault Tolerance

| Attack Scenario                 | FedAvg Accuracy | FedMed+Krum Accuracy |
|--------------------------------|-----------------|----------------------|
| No attack (baseline)           | 93.8%           | 94.1%                |
| 1 malicious client             | 89.2%           | 93.7%                |
| 2 malicious clients            | 78.4%           | 92.9%                |

5. CONCLUSION

FedMed demonstrates that federated learning with differential privacy can
achieve near-centralized accuracy (94.1% vs 95.2%) while providing strong
privacy guarantees. The Krum-based aggregation effectively mitigates Byzantine
attacks. Future work includes extending to multi-class disease classification,
real hospital deployment trials in India, and exploring personalized federated
learning for improved non-IID performance.
